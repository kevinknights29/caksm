\begin{frame}[t]{What this talk argues}
\small

  A trading desk reprices the same multi-asset PDE across a whole book, so the
  cost of \emph{one} solve sets valuation throughput. That solve is dominated
  by \textbf{moving data}, not arithmetic, which is what
  \emph{communication-avoiding} methods target.

  \vskip0.3em
  \begin{alertblock}{}<2->\footnotesize
    \centering
    The question is not \emph{how} to avoid communication, but
    \textbf{when it is worth doing.}
  \end{alertblock}

  \vskip0.4em
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.60\textwidth}
    \begin{enumerate}\scriptsize
      \item<3-> \textbf{The problem and the method}\\
            the operator, and one exponential action per step
      \item<3-> \textbf{Where the time goes}\\
            profiling, the two CA mechanisms, and a null result
      \item<4-> \mine\ \textbf{The regime map}\\
            two a-priori coordinates that predict the opportunity
      \item<4-> \mine\ \textbf{Does it pay?}\\
            a distributed CA integrator on four GPUs
    \end{enumerate}

    \column{0.38\textwidth}\footnotesize
    \begin{block}{\footnotesize Method in one line}<4->\footnotesize
      Predict the opportunity \emph{before} building the kernel, then measure
      whether the prediction holds.
    \end{block}

% -----------------------------------------------------------
% SLIDE 1  |  The operator
% -----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{The problem: one operator, solved over and over}
\small

  Three-asset Black--Scholes in log prices, backward in $\tau = T-t$, for a
  \textbf{basket call} and a \textbf{rainbow min-call}:
  \vskip-0.4em
  \begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial \tau}
      = \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\!\sum_{d,d'}\!\rho_{dd'}\sigma_d\sigma_{d'}
        \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_d \partial x_{d'}}}_{\text{diffusion}+\text{correlation}}
      + \underbrace{\sum_d\!\left(r-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_d^2\right)\!
        \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_d}}_{\text{convection}}
      \underbrace{-\,r u}_{\text{reaction}} + \;b(\tau)
    \tag{1}
  \end{equation*}

  \onslide<2->
  \vskip0.3em
  Second-order central differences on a uniform grid, $N=n^3$ unknowns:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\mat A$ carries \textbf{19 nonzeros per row} and $O(N_1N_2)$
          bandwidth, which rules out banded direct solvers at scale.
    \item Convection is skew-symmetric, so $\mat A$ is
          \textbf{non-symmetric and non-normal}.
    \item Stiffness grows as $\|\mat A\|\sim O(n^2)$.
  \end{itemize}

  \onslide<3->
  \vskip0.3em
  \begin{exampleblock}{}\footnotesize
    \centering
    Production resolution is $n=61$: \textbf{226{,}981 unknowns},
    4.23\,M nonzeros.\\[0.15em]
    Every timing in this talk is for the \textbf{basket call}, the more
    expensive of the two.
  \end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

% -----------------------------------------------------------
% SLIDE 2  |  The method
% -----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{The method: one exponential action per time step}
\small

  Discretizing space only leaves time continuous: the PDE becomes a large
  linear system of ODEs, one equation per grid point.
  \vskip-0.4em
  \begin{equation*}
    \frac{d\vect u}{d\tau} = \mat A\vect u + \mat B\vect s(\tau),
    \qquad \vect u(0)=\vect u_0 \quad(\text{the payoff}).
    \tag{2}
  \end{equation*}

  \onslide<2->
  \vskip0.3em
  For the basket, the payoff and the far-field boundary forcing are
  \vskip-0.4em
  \[
    \vect u_0(\vect x)=\max\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_d w_d e^{x_d}-K,\ 0\Big),
    \qquad
    \vect s(\tau)=\big[\tfrac{\tau^2}{2},\;\tau,\;1\big]^{\mathsf T} .
  \]

  \onslide<3->
  \vskip0.3em
  The forcing is polynomial, so $\vect s'=\mat K\vect s$ with $\mat K$
  nilpotent. Stacking the two states absorbs it \emph{exactly}, and (2) loses
  its forcing term altogether:
  \vskip-0.4em
  \begin{equation*}
    \widetilde{\mat A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mat A&\mat B\\ \mat 0&\mat K\end{bmatrix}
      \in\mathbb{R}^{(N+3)\times(N+3)},
    \quad
    \widetilde{\vect v}=\begin{bmatrix}\vect u\\ \vect s\end{bmatrix}
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \frac{d\widetilde{\vect v}}{d\tau}=\widetilde{\mat A}\widetilde{\vect v} .
    \tag{3}
  \end{equation*}

  \onslide<4->
  \vskip0.3em
  One step from $\tau_k$ is then \emph{one matrix-exponential action} on $\widetilde{\vect v}_k$, approximated in the
  Krylov subspace $\mathcal K_m(\mat G,\widetilde{\vect v}_k)$,
  $\mat G = h\widetilde{\mat A}$:
  \vskip-0.4em
  \begin{equation*}
    \vect u_{k+1}=e^{\mat G}\widetilde{\vect v}_k
    \;\approx\;
    \beta\,\mat V_m\, e^{\mat H_m}\vect e_1 ,
    \qquad \beta=\|\widetilde{\vect v}_k\| .
    \tag{4}
  \end{equation*}

  \vskip0.1em
  \centerline{\footnotesize Call this solver \textbf{KSM-EI}: a
              \textbf{K}rylov \textbf{s}ubspace \textbf{m}ethod used as an
              \textbf{e}xponential \textbf{i}ntegrator.}

\end{frame}

% SLIDE 7  |  How the coordinates are built

\begin{frame}[t]{How the coordinates are built}
\small

  \vskip0.4em
  \textbf{$\Rv$, from the problem.} Working-set bytes implied by $N$, the
  sparsity pattern and the measured Krylov dimension $m$, over the last-level
  cache actually available to the team:
  \vskip0.1em
  \[
    \Rv = \frac{\text{bytes}(N,\ \mathrm{nnz},\ m)}{C_{\text{LLC}}(P)} .
  \]

  \onslide<2->
  \vskip0.6em
  \textbf{$\Rh$, from a calibration instrument.} An isolated scalar all-reduce
  with no application work, fitted over the tree levels it crosses:
  \vskip0.1em
  \[
    \Rh = \frac{\tred(P)}{W_{\text{local}}} ,
    \qquad
    \tred(P) = t_{\text{intra}}L_{\text{in}}(P)
             + t_{\text{cross}}\min\!\big(L_{\text{x}}(P),\,L^{\star}\big) .
  \]
  \vskip0.1em
  \begin{center}\scriptsize
  \begin{minipage}{0.94\textwidth}\centering
    $W_{\text{local}}$, compute between reductions;\hskip0.7em
    $t_{\text{intra}},t_{\text{cross}}$, cost of one tree level inside and
    across a cache domain;\hskip0.7em
    $L_{\text{in}},L_{\text{x}}$, how many levels of each kind the tree
    crosses;\hskip0.7em
    $L^{\star}$, fitted depth beyond which concurrency hides the cost.
  \end{minipage}
  \end{center}

  \vskip0.4em
  \begin{alertblock}{}<3->\footnotesize
    \centering
    \textbf{Raising the cost of a reduction is what moves an operator right,
    and that is a property of topology.}
  \end{alertblock}

\end{frame}

            worth measuring, without timing the solver.
      \item One system where the corner is unreachable and one where more
            participants reach it, on the same definitions.
      \item A distributed CA integrator, \textbf{1.78--1.80$\times$} faster on

            \textbf{5.8--6.4$\times$} faster than smoothed ADI at equal

% A3  |  Numerical admissibility
% -----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Appendix: is the Krylov block numerically admissible?}
\small

  A monomial block $[\vect v,\mat A\vect v,\ldots,\mat A^{q-1}\vect v]$ loses
  conditioning fast, so CholeskyQR2 needs a guard.

  \vskip0.4em
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}\footnotesize
    \textbf{In practice.} The solver narrows the block whenever
    $\kappa_2(\mat Z_q) > u^{-1/2}\approx9.5\times10^{7}$.

    \smallskip
    \textbf{In theory.} CholeskyQR2's sufficient condition is
    dimension-dependent,
    \vskip-0.2em
    \centerline{\scalebox{0.86}{$\displaystyle
      \delta = 8\kappa_2(\mat Z_q)\sqrt{(N{+}3)qu+q(q+1)u}\le1 ,$}}
    \vskip0.1em
    which allows only $\kappa_2\lesssim1.25\times10^{4}$ at our size. The
    measured block reaches $6.0\times10^{6}$, so $\delta\approx482$: the
    theorem is \textbf{inconclusive here, not violated}.

    \smallskip
    \textbf{So the claim rests on} the guard, a successful factorization, the
    orthogonality measured on the right, and referee agreement.

    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \centerline{\footnotesize\textbf{Orthogonality loss, $n{=}61$, $m{=}8$, $q{=}4$}}
    \vskip0.2em
    \begin{center}\scriptsize\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}\toprule
      \textbf{Method} & $\|\mat I-\mat Q^{\mathsf T}\mat Q\|$ & \textbf{Time} \\\midrule
      CholeskyQR2 & $4.0\!\times\!10^{-14}$ & $1.00\times$ \\
      TSQR        & $3.5\!\times\!10^{-14}$ & $3.06\times$ \\
      Block GS, reorth. & $6.5\!\times\!10^{-14}$ & $1.24\times$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \vskip-0.1em
    \centerline{\scriptsize lower is better in both columns}
    \vskip0.4em
    \centerline{\footnotesize\textbf{Width admitted by the guard}}
    \vskip0.2em
    \begin{center}\scriptsize\setlength{\tabcolsep}{6pt}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}\toprule
      \textbf{Basis} & $n{=}31$ & $n{=}61$ \\\midrule
      Monomial  & \textbf{4} & \textbf{4} \\
      Newton (Leja) & 3 & 3 \\
      Chebyshev & 4 & 3 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

                reductions}

% A4  |  Why the monomial basis is enough
% -----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[t]{Appendix: why the monomial basis is enough}
\small

  \vskip0.1em
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.56\textwidth}
    \vskip-0.6em
    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pres_basis_conditioning.png}
    \end{figure}
    \vskip-0.9em
    \centerline{\scriptsize Basket, $n{=}61$. Each line stops at the widest}
    \centerline{\scriptsize block that basis certifies (filled marker).}

    \column{0.41\textwidth}\footnotesize
    \textbf{$m$ stays small.} The endpoint defect is met at a mean
    $m\approx8$, so the recurrence never needs $q>4$.

    \smallskip
    \textbf{Small $q$ is the safe regime.} Monomial conditioning is only a
    problem for wide blocks. At $q\le4$ it stays under the guard, and grows
    \emph{more slowly} than either alternative.

    \smallskip
    \textbf{The alternatives do not pay.} Newton and Chebyshev exist to hold
    conditioning down at large $q$. Here they are worse at every width,
    certify one column fewer, and need spectral estimates the monomial does
    not.
  \end{columns}

  \vskip0.4em
  \begin{exampleblock}{}\footnotesize
    \centering
    Stability here comes from $m$ being small, not from a clever basis.
  \end{exampleblock}

\end{frame}

    and the three-component forcing tail loses its digits. A power-of-two
    similarity rescaling, $\eta=2^{-36}$, brings $\|\mat B\|_1$ to $0.603$ and
    the tail error to $4.41\times10^{-13}$, and the run then passes.
  \end{block}
